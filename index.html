Slow-release Silicon Amendment from Manganese Slag and Phosphogypsum: Release Performance, Metal Immobilization and Soil Fertility Improvement.CORE9

Abstract
The large-scale stockpiling of solid waste, such as electrolytic manganese residue (EMR) and phosphogypsum (PG), threatens both environmental and agricultural sustainability due to soil nutrient loss and heavy metal pollution. This study developed a novel slow-release silicon amendment (S/CRF) using EMR as a silicon source and PG as a controlled-release matrix via mechanochemical-thermal activation with biomass and sodium carbonate. The process effectively transformed inert SiO2 into soluble silicates, achieving silicon content that meets national standards. The controlled release was regulated by lipid barriers and dissolution channels, with a release duration of up to 170 days. In soils contaminated with lead and cadmium, 5% S/CRF-1 application reduced weakly acidic Cd and Pb by 2-10% and 13-18%, respectively, and increased their residual forms by 7% and 6%. Soil pH, cation exchange capacity, and essential nutrients (ammonium nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium) were significantly improved, while beneficial microbial communities were enriched. TCLP tests confirmed heavy metal leaching concentrations met safety standards. This study highlights a novel approach for utilizing industrial waste to remediate contaminated soil and improve fertility.

Abstract
This study investigated the effects of rice straw biochar produced at 450 °C (RBC450) and 700 °C (RBC700) on Pb immobilization, soil properties, and the physiological and molecular responses of Wedelia trilobata (L.) grown in Pb-contaminated soil. Biochar was applied at 1%, 2.5%, and 5% (w/w) to soil contaminated with 150 mg kg−1 Pb. Changes in soil physicochemical properties, Pb availability and accumulation in plant tissues, physiological performance, oxidative stress indicators, antioxidant enzyme activities, and antioxidant-related gene expression were evaluated. Both biochars reduced soil-available Pb and Pb accumulation in roots and shoots, with RBC700 showing greater Pb immobilization. Biochar application also improved soil pH, electrical conductivity, organic carbon, nitrate nitrogen, and available phosphorus. In plants, biochar enhanced catalase and peroxidase activities while reducing malondialdehyde accumulation and reactive oxygen species production, indicating alleviation of Pb-induced oxidative stress. qRT-PCR analysis further showed modulation of SOD-, POD-, and CAT-related antioxidant responses under Pb stress, with RBC450 treatment strengthening antioxidant defense at the transcriptional level. Overall, rice straw biochar reduced soil-extractable Pb and modified soil physicochemical properties and antioxidant responses in W. trilobata, with RBC700 showing greater effectiveness in decreasing Pb extractability. However, these changes did not consistently restore all physiological parameters under Pb stress.

Abstract
Continuous cultivation and fertiliser inputs restructure the spatial distribution of soil nutrient pools and drive the evolution of the underlying soil microenvironment. To elucidate the effects of long-term agricultural interventions on nutrient dynamics and crop productivity in farmland, this study systematically evaluated the long-term ecological effects of fertilisation strategies based on a 46-year long-term wheat–soybean rotation experiment. Twelve fertilisation treatments, including an unfertilised control, straw return, organic manure, and different chemical fertiliser rates alone or in combination with organic amendments, were established. The physicochemical properties of non-rhizosphere soil were systematically determined, and metagenomic sequencing was used to characterise the bacterial community’s diversity and structure. Long-term excessive dependence on chemical fertilisers severely disrupted the soil chemical balance, as indicated by the excessive accumulation of available phosphorus, different available potassium levels among treatments, and decline in pH, increasing the environmental risks associated with agricultural production. The manure-only and manure plus low-rate mineral treatments had more available potassium and showed soil surface features of unverified composition in SEM images. Redundancy analysis revealed that the soil pH, available phosphorus, and available potassium were the principal physicochemical environmental factors associated with differences in non-rhizosphere bacterial communities. Structural equation modelling indicated that although high-rate chemical fertilisation directly promoted wheat yields by increasing available phosphorus, it indirectly suppressed non-rhizosphere microbial diversity by decreasing the soil pH, potentially threatening the long-term sustainability of agricultural productivity. Overall, optimising the fertiliser composition and adopting combined organic and mineral fertilisation are important for balancing soil nutrients, maintaining microbial ecological stability, and achieving sustainable agricultural development.

Abstract
Biochar performance in alkaline, low-fertility soil depends on feedstock, pyrolysis temperature, and application rate, yet the effects of mining-affected Artemisia biomass remain poorly characterized. Artemisia (SBC) and maize straw (BC) biochars produced at 400, 500, and 600 °C were applied at 1% or 5% (w/w) in a 40-day ryegrass pot experiment. We measured plant growth, chlorophyll, antioxidant enzyme activities, plant tissue Fe, Mn, Cu, and Zn, and soil pH, organic carbon, enzyme activities, and diethylenetriaminepentaacetic acid (DTPA)-extractable micronutrients. At 1%, SBC600 increased plant height and root length by 60.98% and 43.10%, respectively, versus the unamended control; SBC500 increased total chlorophyll by 145.71%. Converting this locally available mining-area weed into biochar provides a waste-to-resource strategy that simultaneously manages metal-bearing biomass and reshapes the soil micronutrient profile: the Artemisia biochar was intrinsically richer in Fe, Mn, and Cu but lower in Zn than the conventional maize straw biochar, revealing a feedstock-specific nutrient signature. These short-term, concentration-based results demonstrate clear treatment-dependent benefits, although they do not yet quantify the source or transfer of elements or establish long-term environmental safety.
Lead Release and Contamination Risks Associated with Soil Application of Lead-Loaded BiocharsCORE3
Authors: Zheng-Feng An, Chong Li, A. El-Naggar, M. Naeth, S. Chang
Venue: Land
Published: 2026-11-05
Abs · PDF
Abstract
Biochar is increasingly used in environmental applications, such as remediating soils and water contaminated with toxic elements, because of its high adsorption capacity. However, the environmental fate of toxic elements in spent biochar if used as a soil amendment is poorly understood. In this study, wheat straw and cattle manure biochars, with their lead (Pb)-loaded counterparts (spent biochars) prepared in the laboratory, were applied to a Black Chernozemic soil in an 8-week incubation experiment to evaluate the extractability of Pb in the soil–biochar mixtures and the associated risk of Pb contamination. Pb loading lowered biochar pH by 3.70 units for the wheat straw biochar (from 9.91 to 6.21) and by 3.14 units for the cattle manure biochar (from 10.98 to 7.83), corresponding to approximately 5000-fold and 1400-fold increases in H+ activity. Both spent biochars increased organic acid-extractable Pb (phytoavailable) in the soil–biochar mixtures relative to the control, but to different degrees. Pooled across sampling times (geometric means), organic acid-extractable Pb was 20.3 mg kg−1 under the spent wheat straw biochar and 1.7 mg kg−1 under the spent cattle manure biochar, compared with 0.19 mg kg−1 in the control; potentially mobile Pb followed the same order. Between weeks 4 and 8, the potentially mobile Pb increased under both spent biochars, markedly under the spent cattle manure biochar, whereas phytoavailable Pb declined significantly in the control and the two pristine biochar treatments, indicating that the two operationally defined Pb pools changed differently over time. No significant differences in soil pH or electrical conductivity were detected among treatments after the incubation, and treatment mean pH differed by at most 0.12 units. Bulk soil pH is therefore unlikely to explain differences between the two spent biochars. These results do not show which biochar binds Pb more strongly because the amount of Pb retained by each biochar was not quantified; they do show that, under the conditions tested, land application of Pb-loaded biochar can increase extractable Pb in soil, that the magnitude of this increase differed between the two feedstocks, and that this risk should be assessed before spent biochars are used as a soil amendment.

Venue: Journal of Fungi

Abstract
Thelephora ganbajun is a highly valued edible ectomycorrhizal fungus, but the relationships between its natural distribution and growth and soil environmental conditions remain poorly understood. In this study, mycosphere soils and adjacent control soils were collected from seven geographic sites in Yunnan Province, China. Soil physicochemical properties, enzyme activities, untargeted metabolomics, and bacterial and fungal amplicon sequencing were integrated to characterize geographic variation in the belowground environment and its associations with fruiting-body metabolic phenotypes. Nutrient levels and enzyme activities differed between mycosphere and control soils, and the magnitude of these differences varied among sites. A total of 5006 metabolites were annotated, including 77 differential metabolites shared across all seven sites, although their directions of change were strongly site-dependent. Bacterial and fungal communities in mycosphere soils exhibited significant geographic differentiation. The first two axes of distance-based redundancy analysis cumulatively explained 50.04% and 56.73% of the variation in bacterial and fungal communities, respectively. Soil pH, alkali-hydrolyzable nitrogen, and available phosphorus emerged as key environmental factors, with the latter two significantly associated with both bacterial and fungal community structure. Procrustes analysis further revealed concordant geographic differentiation of the two microbial communities. The volatile, non-volatile, and free amino acid metabolic phenotypes of fruiting bodies were associated with different components of the belowground environment. These findings reveal geographic variation in the belowground environment of T. ganbajun during a single fruiting season and its associations with fruiting-body metabolic phenotypes, providing candidate environmental indicators for future habitat assessment.

Abstract
A field experiment conducted during the 2023 kharif season at Bilaspur, Chhattisgarh, India, examined rice yield, plant nutrient uptake and selected post-harvest soil chemical properties under eight nutrient-management treatments arranged in a randomised block design with three replications. Treatments comprised an unfertilised control, 100% recommended mineral fertiliser dose (RDF) and combinations of 50% recommended nitrogen dose (RDN) with foliar slurry, cow-urine or vermiwash applications. The greatest recorded grain and straw yields occurred under 50% RDN plus slurry sprays at 30, 60 and 90 days after transplanting (T5; 46.93 and 63.66 q ha−1, respectively), compared with 46.50 and 62.26 q ha−1 under 100% RDF (T2). The untreated control yielded 20.13 and 32.33 q ha−1. T5 showed the highest recorded grain nitrogen uptake (59.13 kg ha−1) and straw potassium uptake (108.23 kg ha−1). The treatment means for soil pH, electrical conductivity and organic carbon varied over narrow ranges, with no established treatment differences in the reported experiment. These observations suggest that repeated slurry foliar applications combined with reduced mineral nitrogen coincided with a high rice yield in this setting, whereas the full mineral fertiliser treatment maintained the highest measured residual soil macronutrient availability. Interpretation is necessarily descriptive because independently verifiable plot-level variation and complete liquid-input characterisation are unavailable.

Venue: Journal of The Electrochemical Society

Abstract
Electrodeposition is a clean, efficient method for separating Al and Fe from coal fly ash acid leachate. This study systematically investigates Fe electrodeposition removal. Thermodynamic and electrochemical analyses confirm theoretical feasibility, Pb, Cu, Ni co-deposit with Fe, while Na, K, Ca, Mg, Li, Zn, Ga, Cr, Ti do not interfere. Increasing temperature and Fe2+ concentration shifts Fe2+/Fe reduction potential positively, promoting deposition. Titanium and lead electrodes suppress hydrogen evolution. High AlCl3 concentration effectively suppresses hydrogen evolution without significantly inhibiting Fe deposition. Kinetics show that both Fe2+/Fe and Fe3+/Fe2+ reductions are diffusion-controlled, with diffusion coefficients ~2.4×10-5 and 1.0×10-6 cm2/s. With increasing applied potential, Fe nucleation changes from progressive to instantaneous, reducing grain size and transforming morphology from irregular flakes to star-shaped pentagonal structures. This work provides electrochemical theoretical support for optimizing Fe electrodeposition removal from coal fly ash acid leachate.

Abstract
Mapping of soils and depicting spatial variability across agro-ecological regions remains underexplored, especially in Nepal’s hilly areas. The current study aimed to assess spatial variability and map key soil properties such as pH, soil organic matter, total nitrogen, available phosphorus, and available potassium across the Kaski district, Nepal. Abstract
Microorganisms play key roles in nutrient/carbon cycling and greenhouse gas fluxes in High-Arctic soils. However, the influence of soil conditions on tundra microbiomes remains poorly understood. Using high-throughput sequencing, we studied bacterial, fungal and micro-eukaryotic communities in surface soil-biocrust matrices and the underlying mineral soil along two environmental gradients in Svalbard. Both layers harboured highly different communities. Gravimetric water content and pH were the most important factors in explaining the variation in surface communities. Cyanobacteriota were the dominant phototrophs in wetter tundra soils, while Chlorophyta dominated drier ones. In mineral soils, soil texture explained community structure for all three groups, together with carbon-content and carbon: nitrogen-ratio for micro-eukaryotes and bacteria. Chloroflexota and Spirotrichea dominated mineral soils with high clay content, Actinobacteriota positively correlated with sand content. The effects of soil texture and water content were also reflected in co-occurrence networks. Surface communities split into two network clusters linked to either drier or wetter tundra. The mineral soil network was much less coherent, and clustering was related to soil texture. Our findings revealed the important interplay between soil layer, toposequence and soil microbiome in the High-Arctic. As precipitation patterns shift, the impact of topography on tundra microbial communities is likely to intensify.

Abstract
Agricultural soils underpin global food security but are increasingly constrained by declining organic matter, erosion, and loss of biological functioning under intensified production and climate stress. Biochar has been widely studied as a soil amendment capable of improving these functions while contributing to long-term carbon storage; however, its adoption remains limited by the absence of operational frameworks that translate soil responses into measurable and scalable outcomes. This work presents a soil-science-led conceptual framework that positions biochar application as a soil-function intervention and links biochar-induced processes to a minimal, field-feasible indicator set. The framework centres on a complementary triad comprising a soil enzyme composite index, microbial community composition metrics, and structural stability (aggregate–erosion resistance), representing biochemical, biological, and physical dimensions of soil function. Rather than exhaustive characterisation, the approach emphasises conservative interpretation, site-specific baselines, and repeatable measurements suited to heterogeneous farming contexts. Carbon storage and improvements in soil condition are treated as parallel but analytically distinct outcome pathways, avoiding conflation of carbon quantity with soil quality. By centring on soil functional change, this framework provides a transparent and operational basis for evaluating the impacts of biochar. It supports the integration of soil condition into emerging nature-positive frameworks without reliance on species-level biodiversity metrics. A soil-function indicator framework is proposed for evaluating biochar-amended agricultural soils. Indicators are framed as proxies of soil functional condition, not direct measures of ecosystem processes. Carbon storage and nature-positive soil outcomes are treated as distinct but complementary pathways. A minimal, field-feasible indicator triad supports biochemical, biological, and structural soil functions. The framework enables transparent, scalable assessment aligned with emerging EU carbon and nature policies.

Abstract
During vertebrate decomposition, a purge of nutrient-rich fluids enters the surrounding soil producing a localized nutrient hotspot. The composition of microbial communities at the soil surface is known to predictably change in response to this influx of decomposition fluids over time. However, the depth to which these compositional changes permeate the soil near a decomposing human cadaver and whether specific microbial members exhibit spatial responses to aboveground decomposition both represent significant knowledge gaps. In this study, we longitudinally sampled forest soils adjacent to decomposing human cadavers and from nearby control sites in 2 cm increments to a depth of 10 cm. Soil samples were collected during purge, post purge, and the dry remains stage of decomposition over three seasons at Sam Houston State University's Southeast Texas Applied Forensic Science facility. We detected season-dependent microbial community responses in the upper 0-4 cm of soils adjacent to decomposition fluids. On average, microbial community diversity decreased and community composition became increasingly different in comparison to nearby control soils. Cadaver soils remained impacted for the duration of the 4.5-month spring trial and 1.5-month summer trial. Terriglobus, Acidisoma, Ignatzschineria, and Pandoraea may represent forensically informative biological markers, as they significantly increased in response to decomposition. We observed a more limited, yet still detectable response in the lower 6-10 cm of soil. In addition to improving our understanding of decomposition ecology, we anticipate that incorporating these findings into forensic investigations could help strengthen postmortem interval model estimation and better inform investigative sampling practices.
