Periphyton-associated combined restoration promotes microbial restructuring and phosphorus cycling in gleyed paddy soilCORE10
Authors: Ying Qin, Jun-Zhuo Liu, Qiu-Yue Wang, Qin Liu, Yong-Hong Wu...
Venue: ISME Communications
Published: 2026-11-09
Abs · PDF
Abstract
Persistent reductive stress and soil acidification impose strong environmental filtering in gleyed paddy soils, suppressing microbial activity and limiting phosphorus (P) bioavailability. Inspired by natural soil-water interface dynamics, we developed a restoration strategy combining slow-release CaO2, periphyton development, and phosphate-solubilizing bacteria (PSB) to alleviate habitat constraints and reactivate endogenous P cycling under field conditions. Slow-release CaO2 provided initial oxidative and alkalizing conditioning, effectively elevating soil pH and reducing total reducing substances (TRS). This initial habitat amelioration was accompanied by periphyton proliferation and a marked reduction in within-treatment community heterogeneity (microbiome convergence). Co-occurrence network analysis revealed topological realignments, with PSB taxa (Bacillus and Pseudomonas) occupying module hubs and periphytic microalgae acting as connectors between photoautotrophic and heterotrophic guilds. Metatranscriptomic profiling demonstrated coordinated upregulation of complementary P-cycling pathways, including pyrroloquinoline quinone (PQQ)-dependent inorganic P solubilization and organic P mineralization (phoD, pqq, phn, and bpp). These functional transcripts were positively correlated with soil pH, phosphatase activities, and available P, but negatively correlated with TRS. Overall, this integrated conditioning strategy alleviated environmental filtering and was associated with coupled multi-guild functional responses, ultimately boosting endogenous P-cycling potential in degraded paddy soils. These findings suggest that a functional complementary framework may help inform microbiome-assisted restoration of constrained agricultural soils.
Basalt Application Alters the Nitrogen Budget of Midwestern U.S. Agricultural Soils through Reduced Nitrous Oxide Fluxes and Increased Nitrogen Leaching.CORE10
Authors: I. Kantola, E. Blanc-Betes, M. Masters, Maria Val-Martin, D. Beerling...
Venue: Environmental science & technology
Published: 2026-11-09
Abs
Abstract
Land-based enhanced rock weathering (ERW)─applying ground silicate rock to soils─is being explored for sequestration of CO2 from the atmosphere, with impacts beyond carbon storage. Weathering of basalt releases base cations increasing alkalinity and soil pH, which we hypothesized would reduce N2O emissions. Repeated applications of basalt to a heavily fertilized annual Zea mays and Glycine max rotation (maize and soybean) and the low-fertilized perennial bioenergy grass Miscanthus x giganteus (miscanthus) in Illinois raised soil pH from 6.6 to 7.1 in maize/soybean and from 5.4 to 6.3 in miscanthus and consistently reduced annual N2O emissions by 16-40% over 7 growing seasons. Although not statistically significant for any given crop, our results indicate an emerging trend of increased N leaching (23% pooled across crops and years), suggesting a potential shift in N loss from the atmosphere to groundwater. Crop yields increased or remained unchanged, indicating enhanced weathering is compatible with conventional farming practices. Regional land model simulations show that ERW deployment across the US Corn Belt could reduce soil N2O emissions by 11% under a moderate deployment pathway and by 24% under a more intensive deployment pathway. The effects of enhanced weathering extend beyond CO2 sequestration and may benefit both greenhouse gas mitigation and bioenergy crop production, with a potential trade-off of increased N mobility in fertile, organic matter-rich US Midwestern soils.

Chemical pretreatments for odorant emission control of poultry byproducts and dynamic reactions during soil application.PROXY2
Authors: Edward T. Drabold, W. Neville, Anna Mayes, Davis Branum, Brendan T. Higgins
Venue: Journal of environmental management
Published: 2026-11-09
Abs · PDF
Abstract
Presently, many meat byproducts are land-applied as fertilizer. Expansion of this practice has led to significant public outcry over odorous emissions. Unlike biosolids or manure application, which have been well studied, land application odor control for mostly undigested meat byproducts has received little attention. In the case of the poultry industry, the most land-applied meat byproduct is dissolved air flotation (DAF) solids. In this work, chemical pretreatments, including addition of agricultural lime, quick lime, acid, or oxidizers were considered for DAF solids odorant emission control of volatile organic compounds (VOCs). VOC odorant emissions were quantified by analytical means (GC/MS), not human perception. The application method for quick lime was also considered - including pre-mixing versus surface application of quick lime after DAF solids. These experiments were conducted in controlled mesocosms with real soil, environmental perturbation to understand moisture effects, and were performed until odor extinction (∼200 h) to capture longer-term behavior. The results revealed that pre-mixed quick lime (pH of 12) removed volatile fatty acid (VFAs) emissions below detection without worsening sulfur-organic emissions (a common issue with liming). Meanwhile, agricultural lime delayed VOC odorant emissions and other chemical treatments either increased emissions or had no significant effect. A follow-up mechanistic study determined that VFA volatilization in mesocosm systems was likely due to percolation of liquid from DAF solids and acidification in the soil. Complete mixing with the acidic soil amplified odorant emissions. This study provides important insights into mechanisms that lead to malodors in meat byproduct land application.

Abstract
Water quality and beach sediment particle-size characterization are essential for assessing pollution, particularly at recreational beaches where contamination may pose human health risks. This study evaluates physicochemical, microbiological, and sediment characteristics at 13 coastal sites in Tijuana and Rosarito, Baja California, Mexico, within the broader San Diego- Tijuana binational environmental context. Conducted through the community science project "One Cast, One Community," supported by the San Diego Foundation. The investigation included three sampling campaigns between September 2023 and February 2024, yielding 39 water samples. Water quality measurements included pH, temperature, redox potential (ORP), zeta potential (pZ), Total Nitrogen (TN), Total Phosphorus (TP), Chemical Oxygen Demand (COD), and microbiological tests for enterococci. High enterococci concentrations (49% of samples over 200 MPN/100 mL) were relevant because people are in direct contact with water at recreational beaches. A weighted mathematical index of physicochemical parameters, the water quality index (WQI), showed that 38 of the 39 samples were in poor to extremely poor condition, attributed to mixed runoff (stormwater and unexpected sewage spills) along the Mexican coast. The beach sediment analysis included particle-size distribution, indicating a predominance of sandy soil and the rainy season's impact on particle-size distribution. The sediment samples showed a trend toward slightly moderate alkalinity. The particle-size distribution results indicated that most samples were classified as poorly graded sand (SP), reflecting a uniform grain-size distribution and high permeability. During the rainy season, larger rocks appeared, suggesting cliff erosion along the coastline.

Published: 2026-11-09

Abstract
Open dumping of municipal solid waste can introduce potentially toxic metals into surrounding soils and may create long-term environmental and public-health concerns. This study assessed the concentrations, contamination status, ecological risk and potential human health risks of selected heavy metals in soils around active municipal waste dumpsites at Osubi (Okpe LGA) and Aladja DSC (Udu LGA), Delta State, Nigeria. Soil samples were collected using established field procedures, and cadmium (Cd), lead (Pb), chromium (Cr), nickel (Ni), copper (Cu) and arsenic (As) were determined using Atomic Absorption Spectrophotometry (AAS). Soil contamination and ecological risk were assessed using the geo-accumulation index (Igeo), contamination factor (CF), degree of contamination (Cdeg), modified degree of contamination (mCd), pollution load index (PLI), ecological risk factor (Er) and potential ecological risk index (RI). Pearson correlation analysis was used to examine relationships among the metals, while non-carcinogenic and carcinogenic risks for adults and children were estimated using the hazard quotient (HQ), hazard index (HI) and lifetime cancer risk (LCR). Nickel and arsenic recorded the highest mean concentrations, whereas cadmium had the lowest. Copper and chromium showed a statistically significant positive correlation (r = 0.534, p = 0.049), suggesting a possible common source or similar behaviour in the soil. Although some localized enrichment was observed, particularly for lead and nickel, all Igeo values were negative and classified the soils as practically uncontaminated. The combined contamination and ecological-risk indices also indicated low contamination and low ecological risk. Based on the reported concentrations, HQ and HI values were below 1 for both adults and children, while the estimated cancer risks were below the stated negligible-risk level. Nevertheless, the continued occurrence of arsenic and nickel indicates the need for preventive monitoring and improved waste-management practices. The study recommends routine soil monitoring, improved waste segregation and recycling, engineered landfill development, and stronger enforcement of environmental regulations around the dumpsites.
Computational investigation of the radical-scavenging and Cu(ii)-chelating mechanisms of a tannic acid modelPROXY2

Venue: RSC Advances
Published: 2026-11-09

Abstract
Tannic acid (TA) is a natural polyphenol with well-established antioxidant properties; however, its large and structurally complex nature makes a detailed computational investigation challenging. In this study, a galloyl-based fragment of TA was used as a representative model to investigate its radical-scavenging and Cu(ii)-chelating properties using density functional theory (DFT). The antioxidant activity was evaluated in aqueous and lipid-like media, showing efficient radical scavenging, particularly in water, where the calculated rate constants were higher than those of Trolox and BHT. Mechanistic analysis identified the B ring as the main reactive site and indicated that radical scavenging can occur through hydrogen atom transfer (HAT) from the deprotonated species, particularly the doubly deprotonated form (TA2−). The Cu(ii)-chelating ability of the modeled TA fragment was also investigated under physiological conditions using thermodynamic calculations and Quantum Theory of Atoms in Molecules (QTAIM) analysis. The results showed that Cu(ii) preferentially coordinates with the deprotonated forms through bidentate binding. Among the species considered, TA2− showed the highest Cu(ii)-binding affinity and, based on its calculated abundance under the conditions studied, was identified as a major contributor to Cu(ii) chelation. Overall, these findings provide molecular-level insight into the antioxidant and Cu(ii)-chelating behavior of a model fragment of TA and the molecular factors that may contribute to its radical-scavenging and metal-binding properties under physiologically relevant conditions.
Toward a holistic assessment of economic performance in agricultural plant productionECO1
Authors: Anders Næss, O. Jakobsen, Erik Hjerde, Ø. Haugerud, Kamran Shalchian-Tabrizi
Venue: Frontiers in Sustainable Food Systems
Published: 2026-11-09
Abs · PDF
Abstract
The European Commission reports that 60 to 70% of agricultural soil is degraded, and if current management continues, it is predicted that 90% will be degraded by 2050. Globally, 33% of all agricultural soil is reported as moderately to highly degraded. Ecological economics is guided by three pillars to maintain long-term ecological and economic balance. The pillars are sustainable scale, efficient resource allocation, and fair distribution. If there is a trade-off between maximizing yield and profitability and the overaching soil health, the scaling of management and economic activity is unsustainable. Over time, the system eventually reaches a critical threshold and a possible collapse. This is far from a fair distribution between humans, regarding future generations and non-humans regarding the soil–plant biota as a living ecosystem that also needs a fair share of the dividends we harvest for regeneration. A shift in how we handle the dividends we receive from nature is needed. Our knowledge system about soil–plant biota needs upgrading, followed by new management and economic assessments. In this article, we elaborate on a more holistic assessment of economic performance in agricultural plant production aligned with the three pillars of ecological economics. To illustrate how the pillars can be applied to a practical problem, we refer to a 4-year field trial cultivating organic wheat with and without the application of compost. In our case, compost is the key to transforming from degradation to regeneration. To ensure no trade-off between yield, profitability, and overall soil health, we also measured mineral nutrient balance in the soil, trends in nutrient balance in the harvest, trends in soil organic matter formation, and trends in biological soil health. With the help of these measurements, we can discern whether the soil–plant biota is in a process of regeneration or degradation. Our field trial shows that it is possible to increase yield and profitability, increase carbon stocks in soil, stimulate soil biodiversity, and regenerate soil if a more holistic assessment of economic performance in agricultural plant production is put into practice.
Bioindicator-based monitoring of heavy metals in agricultural runoff using Spirogyra sp. and visible-near infrared (VNIR) spectral analysisECO1

Venue: Environmental Sciences Europe
Published: 2026-11-09
Abs · PDF
Abstract
Heavy metal water pollution in agricultural ecosystems is a major threat to ecosystem functioning and human health; however, rapid and cost-effective monitoring tools are limited. In this study, freshwater algae, Spirogyra sp., were evaluated as bioindicators of heavy metal contamination using Visible-Near Infrared (VNIR) spectral indices and algal bioaccumulation data from canal and rice paddy systems at Mae Hia Agricultural Research Centre, Chiang Mai, Thailand. Water quality varied among habitats, with pH ranging from 6.8 to 8.8. Rice paddy systems supported higher algal biomass, reaching up to 17.62 g dry weight, compared with canal environments, likely reflecting greater nutrient availability and hydrological stability. Spirogyra sp. bioaccumulated all seven metals tested lead (Pb), copper (Cu), zinc (Zn), aluminium (Al), nickel (Ni), cadmium (Cd), and mercury (Hg) with bioaccumulation factors (BAFs) exceeding 1 at every site; Al reached the highest BAF of 2.35×10 6 L/kg in canal samples, followed by Zn, Pb and Cu (2.2–2.5×10 4 L/kg), confirming strong and active metal uptake from the surrounding water. Strong positive correlations among Pb, Cu, Zn, Al, and Ni in algal tissues (Pearson r = 0.95–1.00) indicated shared non-specific biosorption pathways, while mercury exhibited a distinct accumulation pattern. VNIR spectral indices reliably differentiated Spirogyra sp. from surrounding water. Vegetation- and water-related indices were both negatively correlated with Pb and several co-accumulating metals in algal tissue (Kernel Normalised Difference Vegetation Index, KNDVI r = − 0.88 to − 0.95; Modified Normalized Difference Water Index, MNDWI r = − 0.76 to − 0.85). For log-transformed BAFs, the KNDVI gave the clearest spectral response, decreasing as the bioaccumulation of Al (r = − 0.97), Zn (r = − 0.95), Pb (r = − 0.94), Cu (r = − 0.92) and Ni (r = − 0.92) increased, whereas the Normalised Difference Water Index (NDWI)  increased with Hg bioaccumulation (r = 0.87). VNIR spectroscopy cannot directly measure heavy metal concentrations in water, but Spirogyra sp. showed excellent correlations with several heavy metals, mainly Pb, Cu, Zn, Al, Ni, Cd and Hg, suggesting its potential as bioindicator and bioaccumulative organism in freshwater ecosystems. The biomass of algae was highly related to the increasing concentrations of heavy metals in water. Additionally, the use of VNIR spectroscopy for algae has shown potential for estimating heavy metal concentrations from spectral analysis. As far as we are aware, this is the first report combining VNIR spectroscopy with Spirogyra sp. for heavy metal assessment in agricultural freshwater.
Adapting Citizen Science for Environmental Research in Post-War South Lebanon: Civilian Science in ActionECO1
Authors: Leen Dirani, Rami Zurayk, Adel Ghandour, Nermine Faaour, R. Jaber
Venue: Citizen Science: Theory and Practice
Published: 2026-11-09
Abs · PDF
Abstract
War severely affects ecosystems, yet assessing environmental damage during conflict is constrained by restricted access, safety risks, and institutional disruption. This article examines whether citizen-led soil sampling can produce fit-for-purpose environmental evidence under wartime conditions. To assess war-induced soil contamination in South Lebanon following the 2023–2025 bombardments, the study adopted a citizen science approach adapted to conflict settings, often described as civilian science, in which local residents contribute to environmental observation and data collection when formal monitoring is disrupted. Through coordinated efforts involving farmers, students, and municipal staff, 161 soil samples were collected from 29 bombed villages. Volunteers were trained in simplified, standardized sampling protocols and produced valid georeferenced samples, generating the first community-derived dataset of war-affected soils in Lebanon. Laboratory quality assurance and quality control (QA/QC) validation indicated that the accepted citizen-collected samples were methodologically adequate for identifying contamination patterns in sampled locations. The study supports the procedural feasibility of community-based soil monitoring and its ability to generate usable, spatially explicit evidence where conventional monitoring is not possible. At the same time, the findings are context-specific rather than fully representative of all affected areas. The article offers a bounded methodological contribution on the possibilities and limits of participatory environmental monitoring in conflict-affected settings.
